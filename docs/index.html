AB1751   Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act. 4 criteria

Sharon Quirk-Silva  ·  Introduced

ABCD

AB1751 (Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act) creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhome development on any residential parcel, overriding local single-family zoning without community input.

•Cities lose all discretionary authority — projects meeting state objective standards must be approved regardless of neighborhood character, infrastructure capacity, or local planning
•Eliminates design review and public hearings for qualifying developments; no CEQA review applies
•No impact fee authority: cities cannot charge water, sewer, traffic, or school impact fees for new townhome residents — infrastructure costs shift entirely to existing taxpayers
•Sets a precedent for further ministerial preemption of residential zoning across the state
Action →  CSF should actively oppose; submit formal comment letters and coordinate with planning directors to document infrastructure and service capacity concerns.

SB1116   Planning and zoning: housing development projects: subdivisions. 4 criteria

Anna Caballero  ·  Enrolled

ABCD

SB 1116 amends state planning, zoning, and subdivision law to expand by-right housing approvals and restrict local and HOA authority to condition or deny qualifying housing development projects and subdivisions.

•Amendments to Government Code § 65852.28 likely expand ministerial or streamlined approval pathways, stripping cities of discretionary review and the ability to apply local design, density, or compatibility standards.
•Changes to subdivision law (§ 66499.41) limit cities' authority to impose conditions on qualifying subdivisions, reducing the tools available to ensure projects match local infrastructure capacity and community plans.
•Addition of Civil Code § 714.9 restricts HOA and potentially local restrictions on housing development, further preempting local and community-level land use authority.
•Ministerial approval pathways constrain cities' ability to require infrastructure improvements or impact fee conditions as part of project approval, shifting capacity costs toward existing residents.
Action →  Oppose — bill combines explicit local control preemption, discretion removal, and infrastructure condition restrictions with no mechanism for local communities to ensure development fits their capacity or planning goals.

SB1117   Accessory dwelling units and junior accessory dwelling units. 4 criteria

Sabrina Cervantes  ·  Enrolled

ABCD

SB 1117 amends the state's core ADU/JADU approval statute (Gov. Code §66311.5), expanding the scope of projects that must receive ministerial, by-right approval — stripping cities of zoning, design, and discretionary review authority over qualifying units.

•Preempts local zoning: cities must approve qualifying ADUs/JADUs regardless of general plan designations, local density limits, or neighborhood character standards.
•Eliminates discretionary review: ministerial approval mandate removes public hearings, design review, conditional use permits, and CEQA applicability for covered projects.
•Forces development approvals beyond local choice: cities cannot decline or condition projects that meet state criteria, regardless of community preferences.
•Infrastructure burden: increased unit approvals without restored impact fee or utility improvement authority shifts capacity costs to existing residents.
Action →  Oppose — the bill expands state preemption of local land use authority with no mechanism to restore lost discretion or infrastructure cost recovery.

SB1258   Streamlined housing approvals: hazardous waste sites. 4 criteria

Scott Wiener  ·  Introduced

ABCD

SB 1258 expands the SB 35 ministerial housing approval mandate to hazardous waste sites, stripping cities of discretionary authority to condition or deny housing projects on contaminated land.

•Eliminates local discretionary review — including public hearings, design review, and conditional use permits — for qualifying projects on hazardous waste sites via ministerial approval mandate
•Preempts cities' ability to apply site-specific remediation conditions or sequencing requirements before housing approvals on contaminated parcels
•Removes cities' authority to pace or stage approvals in coordination with state and federal environmental cleanup timelines, potentially forcing approvals before remediation is complete
•Limits cities' ability to impose infrastructure and public health conditions tied to contamination status, shifting risk management responsibility onto local governments without state funding backstop
Action →  Oppose — mandating ministerial approval on hazardous waste sites removes the last layer of local oversight protecting residents from premature development on contaminated land, with no mechanism to ensure remediation is complete before occupancy.

AB2074   Regional transit hub districts: downtown housing developments. 4 criteria

Matt Haney  ·  Enrolled

ABCD

AB 2074 creates a state-mandated by-right approval pathway for housing in designated regional transit hub districts, stripping cities of discretionary zoning, design review, and CEQA authority over qualifying downtown projects.

•Explicit state preemption of local zoning — cities must approve qualifying projects regardless of height limits, density standards, or land use designations in locally adopted general plans and zoning codes.
•Ministerial approval mandate eliminates discretionary review, public hearings, design review, and CEQA applicability for projects in transit hub districts, removing community voice from major downtown development decisions.
•Cities are effectively required to permit development volumes and densities that exceed locally chosen levels, functioning as a de facto builder's remedy in designated downtown transit zones.
•Ministerial processing limits cities' ability to condition approvals on infrastructure improvements, potentially shifting utility, traffic, and capacity costs onto existing residents without adequate fee or mitigation authority.
Action →  Oppose — bill layers strong preemption, discretion elimination, and a de facto development mandate with no meaningful local mitigation authority over infrastructure impacts.
