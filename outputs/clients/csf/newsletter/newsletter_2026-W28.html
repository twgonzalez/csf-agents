Blank spot bills are the oldest trick in Sacramento. Here's what to watch.                                                                                          
Local Control Intelligence
WEEK OF JULY 6, 2026
 

The quietest weeks in Sacramento are sometimes the most dangerous. No bills moved a stage this week — but eight crossed into hearing eligibility, two carry no substantive language yet, and the committees that will decide your city's planning authority are about to convene. The window to act is measured in days, not weeks.

No votes this week.Eight clocks just started.the calendar is the weapon.The legislature produced no floor votes and no author amendments this week — and that stillness is exactly the point. While Sacramento appeared quiet, eight bills crossed their committee hearing eligibility thresholds, with AB1710 eligible in six days on March 7 and AB1751 following on March 12. Hearing eligibility is the moment a bill becomes a live projectile: once a committee notices the hearing, amendments narrow, opposition testimony gets compressed, and the window for formal comment letters closes faster than most cities can convene a council vote. The action this week was entirely procedural, and procedural weeks are how Sacramento moves the ball without anyone watching.

Two bills. No text.Hearing eligibility approaching.gut-and-amend is already loaded.AB2127 and AB2079 both carry empty spot-bill summaries — placeholder shells with no substantive content — and both are approaching their hearing eligibility dates. This is the gut-and-amend pipeline in plain sight: a bill is introduced with a harmless title, parked in committee with boilerplate language, and then loaded with substantive preemption text days before a hearing is noticed. Cities have no effective warning. Among this session's broader spot-bill inventory, SB1090 (titled 'Housing: disaster impacted areas') and AB1924 ('Statewide homelessness prevention strategy') carry the same empty 'An act relating to housing' language — innocuous titles that could absorb major preemption provisions before the ink is dry. The gut-and-amend mechanism is not a loophole; it is a deliberate feature of how state mandate legislation avoids early scrutiny.

AB1751 removes discretionary review.Cities also lose fee authority.your water and sewer budget.AB1751, the Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act authored by Sharon Quirk-Silva, scores at maximum risk across all four criteria and preempts local authority in a way that goes beyond zoning. The bill creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhome development on any residential parcel, which removes discretionary review and eliminates CEQA applicability — but the fiscal exposure runs deeper than land use. Cities cannot charge water, sewer, traffic, or school impact fees for new townhome residents under this bill, executing a direct infrastructure cost-shifting onto existing taxpayers with no state offset. AB1710, eligible for its first hearing on March 7, compounds the threat: it amends the Permit Streamlining Act to deem qualifying housing projects automatically in conformity with local ordinances, stripping cities of the case-by-case discretion they currently use to enforce locally adopted standards. These are not redundant bills — they attack local control through different statutory mechanisms simultaneously.

The session pattern is clear.Each bill closes an escape route.no single veto point remains.Step back from any single bill and the architecture becomes visible. AB2390 narrows the objective standards cities may apply during SB 35 streamlined review. AB2576 expands mandatory ministerial approvals near transit. AB2601 extends by-right pathways across five separate code sections — urban lot splits, ADUs, subdivision maps, and SB 35-style streamlining — in a single vehicle. SB677 tightens the Housing Accountability Act and compresses the appeal rights cities use as a last resort under the Subdivision Map Act. Each bill individually removes one tool cities rely on to condition, modify, or shape development. Taken together, they are a systematic state mandate that forecloses local discretion at every stage of the entitlement process. This isn't about any single project or neighborhood. It is about whether California communities retain any meaningful authority over how they grow — and the answer Sacramento is building, bill by bill, is no.

 
What to Watch
AB1710 — Permit Streamlining: Deemed Conformity
Carrillo
Amends the Permit Streamlining Act to require cities to deem qualifying housing projects automatically in conformity with local ordinances, eliminating discretionary review and creating an unfunded compliance burden on local planning staff.
AB1751 — Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act
Quirk-Silva
Creates a statewide ministerial approval pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, removes all discretionary review, and bans cities from charging water, sewer, or traffic impact fees — shifting infrastructure costs directly to existing taxpayers.
AB2390 — Streamlined Approvals: Objective Standards
Schiavo
Narrows the objective standards cities may apply during SB 35 ministerial review, further limiting the conditions local governments can impose on qualifying projects and expanding the mandatory approval pathway.
AB2576 — Transit-Oriented Development
Harabedian
Expands mandatory ministerial approval near transit stops, overrides local zoning without CEQA or public hearings, and restricts cities from conditioning approvals on utility or infrastructure improvements.
AB2601 — Streamlined Approvals and Subdivisions
Lee
Extends by-right ministerial approval across five separate code sections covering lot splits, ADUs, SB 35 streamlining, and subdivision maps, systematically removing local discretionary review at every stage of the entitlement process.
SB677 — Housing Accountability Act: Subdivision Appeals
Wiener
Tightens the Housing Accountability Act and compresses cities' appeal rights under the Subdivision Map Act, narrowing the grounds on which local governments can deny or condition housing approvals and subdivisions.
AB2074 — Regional Transit Hub Districts
Haney
Creates a state-imposed transit hub district overlay mandating ministerial approval for downtown housing near transit, preempting local zoning, eliminating discretionary review, and preventing cities from requiring developers to fund infrastructure capacity.
AB748 — Preapproved Housing Plans
Harabedian
Forces cities to establish and administer state-designed preapproved housing plan programs with no funding offset, compelling expedited approval of conforming projects and stripping local design review and infrastructure conditioning authority.
SB722 — Transit-Oriented Housing: Excluded Parcels
Wahab
Narrows the excluded-parcels list under the Transit-Oriented Communities statute, expanding the universe of sites subject to mandatory by-right approval that overrides local zoning with no community input and no infrastructure conditioning authority.
SB1117 — Accessory Dwelling Units
Cervantes
Amends the existing ADU ministerial mandate framework in a direction that will further restrict the local standards cities may impose and broaden the category of properties subject to by-right ADU approval.
 
Before You Close This

Six days. One letter can matter.AB1710 becomes eligible for its first committee hearing on March 7. Your planning director should submit formal written opposition to the Assembly Housing and Community Development Committee before that date — identifying specifically how the deemed-conformity mandate would override your city's adopted development standards. This isn't about stopping housing — it's about who decides. A letter from your jurisdiction, on letterhead, entered into the committee record before the hearing is noticed, is one of the few tools that remains.

 

Quiet weeks demand louder responses.Sacramento does its most consequential work in the weeks that look uneventful — when clocks start, spot bills get loaded, and committees begin scheduling hearings no one is watching yet. Your job right now is to be watching. The bills on this list are not hypothetical threats; they are active legislation moving through a system designed to compress the window between introduction and approval. Stay in the calendar.
