Blank spot bills are Sacramento's oldest trick. Here's how they work against your city.                                                                                          
Local Control Intelligence
WEEK OF AUGUST 17, 2026
 

The legislature didn't move a single bill this week. That's not a reprieve — it's a runway. Eight bills reach committee-hearing eligibility within the next 14 days, and two of the most dangerous ones are still blank. Understanding how Sacramento uses the calendar against local government is the intelligence advantage your city needs right now.

Nothing moved this week.That's the whole point.the deadline is the weapon.No bills advanced a legislative stage this week and no amendments were filed — but the action calendar is anything but quiet. Eight bills cross their committee-hearing eligibility thresholds in the next 14 days, with AB1710 (housing ordinances, policies, and standards) becoming eligible for its first committee hearing in six days, on March 7. AB1751 and AB1771 follow five days later. In Sacramento, a quiet week before a cluster of eligibility deadlines isn't a pause — it's the moment before the gate opens. Cities that aren't already tracking these bills are already behind.

AB1710 and AB1751.Different bills, same architecture.local discretion is the casualty.AB1710 directly preempts local authority by barring cities from disapproving or conditioning housing projects based on ordinances or standards not in effect when an application was deemed complete — meaning any update your council adopts mid-review becomes legally unenforceable against the applicant. AB1751, the Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act, goes further: it creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, removes discretionary review entirely, strips CEQA applicability, and eliminates your city's authority to charge water, sewer, or traffic impact fees for qualifying developments. These two bills are not redundant — they operate at different stages of the approval process, and together they close nearly every window cities currently use to shape development to fit local conditions.

AB2127 and AB2079.Still blank. Watch them.preemption language lands without warning.The most immediate procedural threat isn't the bills with text — it's the ones without it. AB2127 (accessory dwelling units) and AB2079 (adaptive reuse) are currently carrying empty spot-bill summaries, meaning substantive preemption language can be inserted into either shell at any point before a hearing is noticed. Sacramento's gut-and-amend mechanism allows authors to replace placeholder content with fully formed preemption statutes days before a committee vote, leaving cities almost no time to analyze the text, brief their councils, or file formal opposition. The infrastructure cost-shifting provisions that appear in AB1751 — where cities cannot require impact fees from new townhome residents — are exactly the kind of language that arrives this way: fully written, strategically timed, and dropped into a bill your staff was already tracking for a different reason.

One session. One direction.Every bill confirms the pattern.who decides is still the question.Pull back from the individual bill numbers and the session pattern becomes unmistakable: AB2576 mandates ministerial TOD approvals near transit while restricting impact fee authority; AB2601 expands by-right pathways across five code sections simultaneously; AB2074 creates a state-mandated approval track for downtown transit-hub projects that removes CEQA review, design review, and public hearings in a single stroke. These aren't parallel experiments in housing policy — they are a coordinated architecture of state mandates that progressively removes discretionary review from local governments and shifts infrastructure costs to existing taxpayers with no replacement funding mechanism. The California Stewardship Fund exists for exactly this moment: to make sure city councils, planning directors, and community members can see the full architecture before it's assembled, not after.

 
What to Watch
AB1710 — Permit Streamlining Act: housing ordinances and standards
Carrillo
Bars cities from applying updated zoning standards to housing applications mid-review, directly preempting local authority to condition or deny projects based on any ordinance enacted after the application was deemed complete.
AB1751 — Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act
Quirk-Silva
Creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, removes all discretionary review, eliminates CEQA, and strips cities of authority to collect water, sewer, or traffic impact fees — shifting all infrastructure costs to existing taxpayers.
AB2576 — Transit-oriented development
Harabedian
Extends mandatory ministerial approvals near transit corridors while amending fee and infrastructure provisions in ways that further restrict what cities can require from qualifying TOD projects.
AB2601 — Streamlined approval and subdivisions
Lee
Expands by-right ministerial approval pathways across five code sections — urban lot splits, ADUs, SB 35-style streamlining, lot splits, and subdivision maps — stripping discretionary review and CEQA applicability in a single bill.
AB2074 — Regional transit hub districts: downtown housing
Haney
Mandates ministerial approval for downtown housing in regional transit hub districts, explicitly preempting local zoning, eliminating design review, public hearings, and CEQA, and forcing density beyond what local plans allow.
SB1117 — Accessory dwelling units and junior ADUs
Cervantes
Amends California's already-preemptive ADU statute in ways likely to expand ministerial approval mandates, further restrict the local standards cities may impose, and widen the category of properties qualifying for by-right ADU approval.
AB2433 — Housing development: density bonus
Alvarez
Amends the Density Bonus Law to expand state-mandated density overrides and development standard waivers that cities must grant regardless of local zoning, while limiting the conditions cities can impose on qualifying projects.
SB1014 — Development projects: preliminary estimate of improvements
Grayson
Requires cities to provide applicants with a binding preliminary estimate of required improvements early in review, potentially locking cities out of imposing additional conditions identified later — an unfunded state mandate on local planning departments.
AB748 — Single-family and multifamily housing: preapproved plans
Harabedian
Compels cities to establish and administer preapproved housing plan programs that create ministerial-leaning approval tracks, reducing local design review and imposing unfunded administrative compliance costs with no opt-out mechanism.
SB677 — Housing financing: joint powers agreements and subdivision map appeals
Wiener
Compresses subdivision map appeal timelines and narrows the grounds on which cities may deny or condition tentative and final maps, constraining local discretionary authority over subdivision approvals.
 
Before You Close This

Six days. Brief your council today.AB1710 becomes eligible for its first committee hearing on March 7. Contact your Assembly member now and ask them where they stand on a bill that makes your city's updated zoning standards legally unenforceable the moment a housing application lands on the counter. This isn't about stopping housing — it's about who decides. Your council adopted those standards. Sacramento is telling you they don't count.

 

Watch the calendar, not the votes.The legislature's quiet weeks are when the eligibility clock runs fastest. The bills that will reshape your city's authority over land use, infrastructure, and community character aren't waiting for a dramatic floor vote — they're sliding toward committee hearings while the calendar does the work. Knowing the mechanism is the first line of defense.
