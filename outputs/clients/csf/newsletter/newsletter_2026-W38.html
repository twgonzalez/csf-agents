WEEK OF SEPTEMBER 14, 2026
 

Sacramento didn't move a single bill this week — and that's exactly when you should be watching most carefully. Eight bills cross their committee hearing-eligibility threshold in the next 14 days, and two of them are still empty vessels waiting to be filled with preemption language no one has seen yet. Quiet weeks aren't rest periods. They're the interval between when a trap is set and when it closes.

Nothing moved this week.That's the tell.eight deadlines, two blank bills.The calendar shows no floor votes, no committee passage, no author amendments filed — a quiet week by every surface measure. What it actually shows is a compressing fuse: eight bills cross their committee hearing-eligibility window within the next 14 days, with AB1710 eligible in six days on March 7 and AB1751 following five days later on March 12. Both score 4 out of 4 on CSF's risk criteria. More urgent still, AB2127 and AB2079 are currently carrying empty spot-bill summaries — substantive preemption language can be inserted into either at any moment before their hearings are noticed, and there will be no advance warning when it happens. The legislature uses quiet weeks to move paper; your job is to read the calendar, not the newswire.

Same mechanism. Different bills.Built to stack.a coordinated dismantling of local review.AB1751 — the Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act — creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, directly preempting local zoning and removing all discretionary review from projects that meet state objective standards. AB1710, the most immediately pressing bill on the calendar, amends the Permit Streamlining Act to prevent cities from applying updated ordinances or standards to applications already in the pipeline — freezing local authority at the moment an application is deemed complete, regardless of what the community learns about a project afterward. Meanwhile, AB2074 layers a by-right approval mandate onto designated regional transit hub districts, stripping cities of design review, CEQA authority, and height or density discretion for qualifying downtown projects. These three bills don't duplicate each other; they cover different project types, different geographies, and different procedural leverage points. Together they close off the discretionary review options that remain after any one of them passes.

Cities absorb the cost.Developers keep the yield.infrastructure cost-shifting, written into statute.The fiscal mechanism embedded across this bill cluster is consistent and deliberate: state mandates eliminate the discretionary review moment at which cities impose impact fees, infrastructure conditions, and utility upgrade requirements. AB1751 explicitly prohibits cities from charging water, sewer, traffic, or school impact fees for new townhome residents — infrastructure cost-shifting encoded directly into the bill text. AB2601 and SB1116, both enrolled, amend core subdivision statutes to limit the conditions cities may place on ministerially approved projects, narrowing the infrastructure negotiation window cities rely on to ensure development pays its proportionate share. SB1014 adds a separate pressure point: it requires cities to provide applicants with a preliminary estimate of required improvements early in the review process, creating the legal groundwork for applicants to challenge infrastructure conditions imposed later as exceeding that early estimate. The cumulative effect is a statutory architecture that approves density while blocking the fee and condition authority cities use to pay for it.

This session has a logic.Spot bills complete it.who decides is the only question.The pattern running through this session is not a collection of independent housing bills — it is a layered replacement of local deliberation with state mandate, proceeding one statutory section at a time. AB748 mandates preapproved plan programs that compress design review. SB677 restructures subdivision map appeal timelines to narrow the window cities have to impose conditions or deny maps. AB2433 expands density bonus law in ways that force cities to approve higher-density projects under state-defined affordability thresholds rather than locally negotiated ones. The spot bills — AB2127, AB2079, and others carrying only boilerplate language right now — represent the next chapter of that story, and the content won't be visible until it's almost too late to respond. CSF's position has not changed and will not: this fight is not about whether California builds housing. It is about whether the people closest to a decision — elected council members, planning commissioners, the residents who will live next to what gets built — retain any meaningful voice in how that decision is made.

 
What to Watch
AB1710 — Permit Streamlining Act: housing conformity
Carrillo
Prevents cities from applying updated zoning ordinances or objective standards to applications already in review, freezing local authority at the application date and removing discretionary leverage mid-process.
AB1751 — Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act
Quirk-Silva
Creates a statewide ministerial approval pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, preempts local zoning, eliminates discretionary review, and bars cities from collecting impact fees for water, sewer, traffic, or schools — shifting infrastructure costs entirely to existing taxpayers.
AB2601 — Streamlined approval and subdivisions
Lee
Expands state-mandated by-right approvals across core housing and subdivision statutes, further restricting the conditions and infrastructure requirements cities may impose on qualifying projects.
SB1116 — Housing development projects: subdivisions
Caballero
Expands ministerial housing and subdivision approvals, restricts HOA and local conditioning authority, and limits cities' ability to impose infrastructure requirements on qualifying projects.
AB2074 — Regional transit hub districts: downtown housing
Haney
Mandates by-right approval for housing in designated downtown transit hub districts, preempting local zoning, eliminating CEQA and design review, and limiting cities' ability to condition approvals on infrastructure improvements.
SB677 — Housing financing: subdivisions: map appeals
Wiener
Compresses subdivision map appeal timelines, narrows grounds for city-imposed conditions and denials, and expands joint powers housing finance authority in ways that can bypass local voter or council approval.
SB1014 — Development projects: preliminary improvement estimates
Grayson
Requires cities to issue preliminary estimates of required onsite and offsite improvements early in review, creating an unfunded administrative burden and a legal baseline applicants can use to challenge infrastructure conditions imposed later.
AB2433 — Housing development: density bonus
Alvarez
Expands state density bonus law, forcing cities to approve higher-density projects under state-defined thresholds and reducing local authority to impose proportional infrastructure conditions on bonus projects.
AB748 — Single-family and multifamily: preapproved plans
Harabedian
Compels cities to establish and administer preapproved housing plan programs, creating state-mandated streamlined approval tracks that compress local design review and impose unfunded administrative obligations with no opt-out.
SB1117 — Accessory dwelling units and JADUs
Cervantes
Expands the scope of ADUs and JADUs that must receive ministerial by-right approval, preempting local zoning and eliminating discretionary review while increasing permitting load without restoring impact fee authority.
