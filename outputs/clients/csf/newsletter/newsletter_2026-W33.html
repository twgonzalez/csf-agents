Spot bills are the sleeper threat. Here's how Sacramento uses them — and why it matters now.                                                                                          
Local Control Intelligence
WEEK OF AUGUST 10, 2026
 

No votes were cast in Sacramento this week. No amendments were filed. That's not calm — that's the quiet before eight bills simultaneously become eligible for committee hearings, and two of them are still blank shells that can be loaded with preemption language hours before a hearing is noticed. The window your city has to prepare is closing faster than the calendar makes it look.

Quiet week. Loaded calendar.Eight deadlines converge fast.the silence is the strategy.Nothing moved in Sacramento this week — no floor votes, no amendments, no committee actions. That's the accurate headline, and it would be easy to read as breathing room. It isn't. AB1710, which preempts local authority over how cities apply their own ordinances to housing projects under the Permit Streamlining Act, becomes eligible for its first committee hearing in six days, on March 7. AB1751, the Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act, follows five days later on March 12. In the same two-week window, six more bills cross their eligibility thresholds — meaning the window for coordinated local opposition to any of them is already narrower than it appears on the surface.

Blank bills. Real teeth.Language drops without warning.your staff won't see it coming.Two bills on the hearing-eligibility list — AB2127 on accessory dwelling units and AB2079 on adaptive reuse — currently carry empty spot-bill summaries: placeholders with no substantive content and no preemption language yet. That's not a sign they're harmless. It's the standard Sacramento mechanism for inserting state mandate language at the last possible moment before a hearing is noticed, giving local governments almost no time to analyze the provisions, brief council members, or mobilize formal opposition. The same pattern applies to SB1090, framed around housing in disaster-impacted areas, and SB1016, titled as a transitional housing bond — both holding in Rules Committee with nothing but boilerplate text. Any of these shells can become a bill that removes discretionary review or shifts infrastructure costs to existing taxpayers before your city attorney has read the first section.

AB1751 still leads the threat.Fee authority is the kill shot.your water, sewer, and traffic budgets.AB1751 has been on this watch list for weeks, and the reason it stays at the top isn't repetition — it's that the infrastructure cost-shifting mechanism it contains is the most direct fiscal threat to California cities in the current session. The bill creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, and it explicitly strips cities of authority to charge water, sewer, traffic, or school impact fees for projects approved under that pathway. That's not a policy disagreement about density — it's a state mandate that forces local governments to extend service capacity to new residents while prohibiting the fees that pay for that capacity. AB2390 tightens the same screw on SB 35 projects, AB2576 applies the same logic near transit corridors, and AB2601 rolls the mechanism across five separate code sections at once. These bills don't operate in isolation. They are compounding layers of the same infrastructure cost-shifting framework.

Pattern is the point.Each bill locks the last.local planning authority is the casualty.SB1014, which requires cities to provide applicants with a preliminary estimate of required improvements early in the review process, looks like a transparency measure — and that framing is exactly how it moves through committee without local government opposition. But the practical effect is that any improvement condition not surfaced in that preliminary estimate becomes harder to enforce later, compressing the window in which cities can protect infrastructure capacity. That's the architecture of this session: spot bills that can be loaded with preemption language at any moment, eligibility windows that cluster to overwhelm local response capacity, and individually modest-sounding bills that each remove one more tool from the local planning director's desk. AB1710 alone or AB1751 alone is a serious problem. Eight bills in fourteen days, with two more that don't yet have text, is a session strategy. The question California Stewardship Fund is asking — and that every city council member should be asking — is who decides how your community grows when all of those tools are gone.

 
What to Watch
AB1751 — Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act
Quirk-Silva
Creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, removes all discretionary review, and prohibits cities from charging impact fees for water, sewer, traffic, or schools — shifting infrastructure costs entirely to existing taxpayers.
AB1710 — Permit Streamlining Act: housing conformity
Carrillo
Amends the Permit Streamlining Act to restrict how and when cities may apply local ordinances and standards to housing projects, effectively locking in standards at the moment of application and preventing cities from enforcing updated or stricter conditions during review.
AB2390 — Streamlined housing approvals: objective standards
Schiavo
Narrows the objective standards cities may apply under the SB 35 ministerial approval pathway, further stripping local discretion over project conditions and accelerating approvals regardless of infrastructure readiness.
AB2576 — Transit-oriented development
Harabedian
Amends the TOD statute to mandate ministerial approval of qualifying housing near transit while restricting the impact fees and improvement conditions cities may impose, leaving existing residents to absorb capacity costs.
AB2601 — Streamlined approvals and subdivisions
Lee
Expands ministerial by-right approval pathways across five code sections — urban lot splits, ADUs, SB 35 streamlining, lot splits, and subdivision maps — removing discretionary review and CEQA applicability from each.
AB2074 — Regional transit hub districts
Haney
Creates a new state-mandated by-right approval framework for downtown housing near transit hubs, preempting local zoning in designated areas and eliminating city discretion over density, design, and infrastructure conditions.
AB2433 — Housing development: density bonus
Alvarez
Expands the state-mandated Density Bonus Law entitlements cities must grant qualifying projects, enlarging the universe of developments cities are legally compelled to approve at densities that override local zoning.
SB1014 — Development projects: preliminary improvement estimates
Grayson
Requires cities to issue preliminary infrastructure improvement estimates early in the approval process, creating a procedural trap that constrains cities from imposing conditions not captured at that early stage — an unfunded mandate dressed as transparency.
SB677 — Housing financing: JPA bonds and subdivision appeals
Wiener
Compresses subdivision map appeal timelines and narrows the grounds for denial while reducing local legislative oversight of JPA housing finance structures, limiting community input at two critical points in the development process.
SB1117 — Accessory dwelling units and junior ADUs
Cervantes
Amends ADU and JADU law in a direction that almost certainly extends ministerial approval mandates or restricts the local standards cities may still impose, deepening a preemption framework that already eliminates most meaningful local review.
