Local Control Intelligence
WEEK OF SEPTEMBER 7, 2026
 

Sacramento didn't move a single bill this week. That's not a pause — it's the calm before a cluster of committee eligibility deadlines hits all at once. Eight bills become hearable within the next 14 days, two of them still carrying blank legislative text that could be swapped for sweeping preemption language before you see it coming. The window to prepare is now, and it is shorter than it looks.

The calendar is the threat.Blank bills, live deadlines.preemption can arrive without warning.Nothing moved in Sacramento this week — no floor votes, no author amendments, no committee hearings. What did move is the clock. AB1710, which the staff watchlist rates 4-out-of-4 on every risk criterion, becomes eligible for its first Housing and Community Development Committee hearing in six days, on March 7. AB1751 and AB1771 follow five days later on March 12. That compressed eligibility cluster matters because committees can notice a hearing with as little as four days' warning — meaning AB1710 could be in a hearing room before the next issue of this newsletter lands in your inbox. If your city hasn't submitted a formal position letter, the window is closing now.

Spot bills don't announce themselves.AB2127 and AB2079 are empty.any language can be dropped in.The procedural mechanism that deserves the most attention this week isn't a headline bill — it's the spot bill. Two bills on the accessory dwelling unit and adaptive reuse watch list, AB2127 and AB2079, currently carry nothing beyond boilerplate printer-stage summaries. Under the gut-and-amend process, an author can replace that placeholder text with substantive preemption language at any point before a hearing is formally noticed — and once the hearing clock starts, public comment windows compress dramatically. AB2127 and AB2079 are not alone: five additional spot bills carry titles ranging from 'Office of Youth Homelessness Prevention' to 'Housing: disaster impacted areas,' each one a legislative vessel that could be filled with zoning preemption, ministerial approval mandates, or infrastructure cost-shifting provisions before the session's next major deadline. Tracking bill titles is not enough; cities need to be watching bill text on a daily basis right now.

AB1751 still leads the risk list.The fee ban is the hidden mechanism.your existing taxpayers absorb the cost.AB1751 — the Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act — removes discretionary review for townhome development on any residential parcel statewide, but the provision that most directly damages city budgets is the one that gets the least attention: cities cannot charge water, sewer, traffic, or school impact fees for qualifying townhome residents. That is explicit infrastructure cost-shifting written into the statute. Every new unit that enters a city's water system, generates traffic, or seats a child in a public school does so without contributing the impact fee revenue that would normally offset those capital and service demands. SB1014, also on the watchlist, compounds this dynamic by requiring cities to issue preliminary infrastructure improvement estimates early in the review process — estimates that applicants can then use to contest conditions imposed later, effectively anchoring and limiting the city's ability to require full mitigation once actual project impacts are better understood. Together these bills don't just preempt local authority over land use; they preempt the fiscal tools cities use to make development pay its own way.

This is a designed sequence.Not coincidence — coordination.who decides is the whole question.The broader session pattern across AB1751, AB1710, AB2601, SB1116, SB1117, AB2074, AB748, and SB677 is not a collection of independent housing bills — it is a layered state mandate architecture. Ministerial approval pathways remove discretionary review at the project level. Subdivision Map Act amendments compress the timeline and narrow the grounds on which cities can condition or deny tentative maps. Density bonus expansions through AB2433 and AB939 lock in state-defined affordability thresholds that override locally negotiated standards. Preapproved plan mandates under AB748 create ministerial-leaning tracks that compress design review for conforming projects. Each bill is designed to be defensible in isolation; together they systematically dismantle the layers of local control that communities use to ensure growth matches infrastructure capacity, neighborhood character, and community plans. California Stewardship Fund's position is not that housing shouldn't be built — it's that the people closest to these decisions should make them, and right now Sacramento is methodically ensuring they can't.

 
What to Watch
AB1710 — Permit Streamlining Act: Housing Conformity
Carrillo
Preempts local zoning authority by barring cities from applying updated ordinances or standards to housing applications already deemed complete, locking in rules at application date and eliminating cities' ability to impose new conditions during review.
AB1751 — Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act
Quirk-Silva
Creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, removes discretionary review entirely, and bans cities from charging impact fees for new residents — shifting infrastructure costs to existing taxpayers by state mandate.
AB2601 — Streamlined Approval and Subdivisions
Lee
Amends core ministerial approval and subdivision statutes to further restrict the conditions cities may impose on qualifying housing projects, expanding by-right approvals that override local zoning and remove CEQA review.
SB1116 — Housing Development: Subdivisions
Caballero
Expands by-right housing approvals and limits cities' authority to condition subdivisions on infrastructure capacity, while restricting HOA and local design standards through a new Civil Code provision.
SB1117 — ADU and JADU Approvals
Cervantes
Amends the state's core ADU approval statute to expand the categories of units that must receive ministerial approval, stripping cities of zoning, design, and discretionary review authority and shifting infrastructure costs to existing residents.
AB2074 — Regional Transit Hub Housing
Haney
Creates a by-right ministerial approval pathway for downtown housing in transit hub districts, explicitly preempting local general plans, specific plans, and zoning designations while eliminating CEQA and design review.
SB1014 — Preliminary Infrastructure Estimates
Grayson
Requires cities to issue preliminary improvement estimates early in project review, creating an unfunded administrative burden and giving applicants a legal foothold to challenge infrastructure conditions that exceed the early estimate.
AB748 — Preapproved Housing Plans
Harabedian
Forces cities to establish and administer preapproved plan programs for single-family and multifamily housing, creating state-mandated streamlined tracks that compress local design review with no reimbursement for the administrative cost.
SB677 — Subdivision Map Act and JPA Bonds
Wiener
Amends subdivision map appeal timelines and joint powers bond authority in ways that compress city review windows, narrow grounds for denial, and reduce local leverage over project terms and infrastructure conditions.
AB2433 — Density Bonus Law Amendments
Alvarez
Amends the Density Bonus Law to expand state-mandated entitlements that override local zoning, substituting Sacramento's density and affordability formulas for locally negotiated standards and community-driven planning decisions.
