Blank bills aren't inactive bills. Here's what Sacramento can drop in before you blink.                                                                                          
Local Control Intelligence
WEEK OF OCTOBER 12, 2026
 

No votes were cast this week. No amendments were filed. And that's exactly when Sacramento is most dangerous — when the calendar is quiet and the deadline clock is loud. Eight bills cross their hearing-eligibility threshold in the next 14 days, two of them still carrying empty placeholder language that can be replaced with hard preemption text the moment a hearing is noticed. This issue is about what's coming, and why the silence this week is not a reprieve.

Nothing moved this week.Eight deadlines approach.the blank bills are the threat.The quietest weeks in Sacramento are often the most consequential ones to miss. No bills advanced a legislative stage and no author amendments were filed — but eight bills cross their hearing-eligibility threshold within the next 14 days, and two of them, AB2127 and AB2079, currently carry empty spot-bill summaries. That means substantive preemption language can be inserted into either at any moment before their hearings are noticed, with no additional public warning. AB1710 becomes eligible for its first committee hearing in six days, on March 7. AB1751 follows five days later. The opening of the hearing window is not a formality — it is the last moment your city has maximum leverage to be heard.

Same mechanism, different bill.Stacked across the calendar.your planning staff can't cover them all.What makes this session distinctive isn't any single bill — it's the assembly-line replication of the same legal mechanism across a dozen vehicles simultaneously. AB1751 creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, removing discretionary review entirely and preempting local authority over design, density, and conditions. AB1710 amends the Permit Streamlining Act to freeze the zoning standards cities can apply the moment an application is deemed complete, stripping cities of the ability to update objective standards mid-review. AB2601 and SB1116 each amend overlapping sections of the Government Code — §65852.28 and §66499.41 appear in both — to expand by-right approval pathways and ministerial subdivision eligibility. When the same statutory sections are being amended by multiple authors in the same session, that is coordination, not coincidence. Your planning director needs to be tracking all of them at once.

The fee ban is buried.The budget hit is not.existing ratepayers absorb the cost.The most consequential provision in AB1751 gets the least attention in bill summaries: cities cannot require impact fees — water, sewer, traffic, school — for new townhome residents on ministerially approved projects. That infrastructure cost-shifting doesn't disappear; it lands on the utility ratepayers and taxpayers already living in your city. SB1014, which requires cities to issue preliminary estimates of required onsite and offsite improvements early in the development review process, adds a second pressure point: if those preliminary estimates carry any binding or estoppel effect — and the full bill text has been flagged for review precisely because of that ambiguity — cities could lose the ability to impose additional infrastructure conditions discovered later in the process. Taken together, AB1751's explicit fee prohibition and SB1014's potential de facto condition freeze create a pincer on the infrastructure authority your city needs to keep existing services solvent as new density arrives.

This is a session pattern.Not an isolated bill.who decides is the only question.The spot bills — AB2127, AB2079, and others still carrying boilerplate 'an act relating to housing' summaries — are not accidents of legislative housekeeping. They are vehicles held open intentionally, available to carry hard preemption language the moment a strategic opportunity arises. SB677 compresses subdivision map appeal windows. AB2433 expands the Density Bonus Law's override of local zoning. AB748 compels cities to build and administer preapproved plan programs at their own expense with no state reimbursement. Each bill, individually, might be defensible on narrow grounds. Together, they constitute a systematic removal of discretionary review, local zoning authority, and infrastructure cost accountability from the governments closest to the communities they serve. The California Stewardship Fund's position is not anti-housing. It is this: the people who live in a community should have a meaningful voice in how it grows — and right now, Sacramento is working methodically to make sure they don't.

 
What to Watch
AB1710 — Permit Streamlining Act: Housing Ordinances
Carrillo
Freezes the zoning standards cities can apply once an application is deemed complete, preempting local authority to update objective standards mid-review and eliminating a critical conditional leverage point.
AB1751 — Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act
Quirk-Silva
Creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, removes all discretionary review, and explicitly bars cities from charging impact fees — shifting infrastructure costs to existing taxpayers.
AB2601 — Streamlined Approval and Subdivisions
Lee
Simultaneously amends zoning streamlining, subdivision, and CEQA statutes to expand ministerial by-right approval, preempting local discretionary review across multiple legal frameworks at once.
SB1116 — Housing Development Projects: Subdivisions
Caballero
Expands state-mandated ministerial approval for housing and subdivision projects, removes cities' ability to impose infrastructure conditions, and adds an unfunded compliance reporting burden.
AB2074 — Regional Transit Hub: Downtown Housing
Haney
Mandates by-right approval for downtown housing in transit hub districts, explicitly preempting local zoning and eliminating CEQA, design review, and public hearings for qualifying projects.
SB1361 — Transit-Oriented Housing Developments
Durazo
Expands the mandatory by-right approval regime for housing near transit corridors, removing local discretion over density, design, and infrastructure conditions on affected parcels.
SB1014 — Preliminary Estimate of Required Improvements
Grayson
Requires cities to issue early preliminary estimates of infrastructure improvements that may carry binding effect, potentially foreclosing cities' ability to impose additional conditions discovered later in review.
AB2433 — Housing Development: Density Bonus
Alvarez
Expands California's Density Bonus Law, which by design overrides local zoning to compel greater density and restricts cities' ability to impose infrastructure conditions on qualifying projects.
AB748 — Preapproved Housing Plans
Harabedian
Compels cities to establish and administer preapproved plan programs for housing, creating ministerial-leaning approval tracks that compress local design review while imposing unfunded administrative costs.
SB677 — Subdivision Map Act: Bond Approvals and Appeals
Wiener
Modifies subdivision map appeal procedures and JPA bond rules in ways that compress local discretionary review windows and expand state-sanctioned financing vehicles operating outside normal city deliberation.
 
Before You Close This

Call or email your Assembly member's district office before that date and ask them directly: do you support stripping cities of the ability to apply updated zoning standards during active permit review? This isn't about stopping housing — it's about who decides. Your council members and planning commissioners should be making that call today, not the morning a hearing is noticed.
