Spot bills let Sacramento write the preemption after the deadline pressure is already built.                                                                                          
Local Control Intelligence
WEEK OF AUGUST 24, 2026
 

No votes were cast in Sacramento this week. No amendments were filed. But the calendar moved anyway — and that's the story. Eight bills cross their committee hearing eligibility threshold in the next 14 days, and two of them currently contain no substantive language at all. Understanding how that mechanism works is worth more than any single vote.

Nothing moved this week.The clock did.eight eligibility deadlines, fourteen days.The quietest weeks in Sacramento are sometimes the most instructive. No bills advanced a legislative stage, no author amendments were filed, and the hearing calendar looked almost calm — until you count the eligibility windows closing in. Eight bills on the local control watch list become eligible for their first committee hearings within the next 14 days. AB1710, which would freeze the zoning standards cities can apply mid-review by amending the Permit Streamlining Act, crosses its threshold in six days on March 7. AB1751 — the Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act — follows five days later on March 12. The bills haven't moved. Their deadlines have.

Two bills. No text.Deadlines already set.the gut-and-amend window is open.AB2127 and AB2079 are currently carrying empty spot-bill summaries — placeholder shells with hearing eligibility dates already baked in. That's not an oversight; it's a procedural strategy. Under California's gut-and-amend process, a bill's author can substitute entirely new substantive language at any point before a hearing is noticed, and the compressed timeline that follows gives opponents almost no runway to respond. The same pattern applies to five additional spot bills tracked this week: AB1899, SB1016, AB1924, SB1090, and AB1795 each carry only a printer-stage summary and a title that doesn't telegraph their final content. Any one of them could absorb preemption language on a week when your planning director isn't watching.

The fees disappear first.Then the budget follows.existing taxpayers absorb the gap.The fiscal mechanism inside the high-risk bills on this week's list is consistent enough to be a pattern, not a coincidence. AB1751 explicitly prohibits cities from charging water, sewer, traffic, or school impact fees on qualifying townhome projects — meaning every unit approved under its ministerial pathway arrives with infrastructure costs that local government must absorb or pass to existing residents. AB2601 compounds this across five separate code sections, stripping cities of the ability to condition approvals on capacity improvements for any project that qualifies under its expanded ministerial tracks. AB2433 extends the same logic through the Density Bonus Law, where state-mandated concessions already limit what cities can require. These bills don't just preempt local authority over land use — they preempt the fiscal tools cities use to make growth pay for itself.

Same mechanism. Different bill numbers.The session pattern is set.who decides is the only question left.Stack the watch list and the architecture is plain: ministerial approval removes discretionary review, discretionary review removal blocks infrastructure conditions, blocked conditions shift costs to existing taxpayers, and shifted costs erode the local budget capacity that makes self-governance possible. SB1361 and AB2074 run this playbook in transit corridors. AB1710 runs it during the permit window itself, freezing applicable standards at the moment of application so cities can't update requirements even when conditions change. SB677 narrows the appeal rights cities use to shape subdivision outcomes. The California Stewardship Fund's position is not that these projects shouldn't be built — it's that the people who will pay for the infrastructure, send their children to the affected schools, and drive the impacted roads deserve a seat at the table before the approval is stamped.

 
What to Watch
AB1710 — Permit Streamlining Act: Housing Conformity
Carrillo
Freezes the zoning standards cities may apply mid-review, removing the ability to update objective requirements after an application is deemed complete and preempting local authority at the moment it matters most.
AB1751 — Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act
Quirk-Silva
Creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, removes all discretionary review and CEQA applicability, and explicitly bars cities from charging impact fees — shifting infrastructure costs entirely to existing taxpayers.
AB2601 — Streamlined Approval and Subdivisions
Lee
Expands ministerial by-right approval tracks across five code sections covering lot splits, ADUs, SB 35 streamlining, and subdivision maps, stripping cities of the discretionary conditions and CEQA tools used to tie approvals to infrastructure capacity.
SB1117 — Accessory Dwelling Units and JADUs
Cervantes
Amends California's existing ADU preemption statute in a direction that is expected to further restrict local standards and expand mandatory ministerial approvals beyond what cities have locally chosen to permit.
AB2074 — Regional Transit Hub Districts: Downtown Housing
Haney
Creates a new by-right approval pathway for downtown housing in transit hub districts, overriding local general plans and zoning while blocking cities from conditioning approvals on infrastructure improvements.
SB1361 — Transit-Oriented Housing Developments
Durazo
Expands mandatory ministerial approvals under California's transit-oriented development framework, removing city discretion over design, density, and infrastructure conditioning across an expanded range of transit corridors.
AB2433 — Housing Development: Density Bonus
Alvarez
Expands the Density Bonus Law to compel cities to grant higher density entitlements and developer concessions beyond local zoning, narrowing the conditions and objective standards cities may apply and shifting incremental infrastructure costs to existing residents.
SB677 — Housing Financing: Joint Powers and Subdivision Map Appeals
Wiener
Restricts local appeal and disapproval authority over subdivision maps and narrows the grounds cities may use to deny or condition tentative and final maps, compressing the procedural tools cities rely on to shape land division outcomes.
AB748 — Single-Family and Multifamily Housing: Preapproved Plans
Harabedian
Mandates that cities establish and administer preapproved housing plan programs, creating ministerial-leaning approval tracks that remove local discretion over design and land use standards with no state reimbursement for the compliance burden.
SB1014 — Development Projects: Preliminary Improvement Estimates
Grayson
Requires cities to produce preliminary estimates of required onsite and offsite improvements early in the development review process, adding an unfunded state-mandated administrative obligation to already-stretched local planning departments.
