Local Control Intelligence
WEEK OF JULY 20, 2026
 

The Capitol was quiet this week — no votes, no amendments, no floor action. Don't mistake quiet for safe. Eight bills cross their committee eligibility threshold in the next 14 days, two of them carrying nothing but a title and a blank body that authors can fill with preemption language at any moment. A quiet week is when Sacramento does its most dangerous procedural work, and it's when your attention matters most.

No votes. No amendments.Eight deadlines approaching.the window is closing.Nothing moved a legislative stage this week — but the calendar moved. AB1710, which adds Section 65958 to the Government Code and requires cities to deem qualifying housing projects automatically in conformity with local ordinances, becomes eligible for its first committee hearing in six days on March 7. AB1751 and AB1771 follow five days later on March 12. Altogether, eight bills cross their hearing-eligibility threshold within the next 14 days. In Sacramento's calendar logic, 'eligible' is one step from 'noticed,' and 'noticed' is one step from 'voted.' The clock is running whether the hearing room is full or empty.

Deemed conforming.Cities can't say no.your ordinances become advisory.AB1710 is the sharpest instrument in this two-week window. By mandating deemed-conformity for qualifying housing projects, it preempts local authority to apply development standards on a case-by-case basis — no discretionary review, no design conditions, no enforceable local zoning — and it does so through the Permit Streamlining Act, a statute cities already find difficult to push back against. AB1751 runs the same mechanism in a different wrapper: it creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhome development on any residential parcel, removes discretionary review entirely, strips CEQA applicability, and — critically — prohibits cities from collecting water, sewer, traffic, or school impact fees from new townhome residents. These two bills are not redundant; they're additive. AB1710 resets the conformity baseline, and AB1751 locks cities out of the one fiscal lever — impact fees — that might otherwise let them absorb the growth these state mandates require.

Blank bills. Real deadlines.Authors choose the content.infrastructure cost-shifting by surprise.The less-reported danger this session lives inside the spot bills. AB2127 and AB2079 — both on the eligibility calendar within 14 days — currently carry empty summaries, as do five others on the broader watchlist: AB1899, SB1016, AB1924, SB1090, and AB1795. A spot bill in California's legislative process is a placeholder: it holds a bill number, a committee assignment, and an eligibility date while the author finalizes the substantive language. That language can be inserted at any point before the hearing is noticed — meaning a bill titled 'Office of Youth Homelessness Prevention' or 'Fire insurance: residential property' can arrive in committee carrying a full ministerial approval regime, a state mandate stripping discretionary review, and infrastructure cost-shifting provisions your city had no advance warning to analyze. The threat isn't theoretical. It's procedural, and it's already scheduled.

Not one bill.A compounding architecture.who decides how California grows.Zoom out from any single bill and the session pattern becomes legible. AB2390 tightens the conditions under which cities may push back on SB 35-qualifying projects. AB2576 further restricts infrastructure conditions on transit-oriented development. AB2601 expands ministerial approval pathways across five separate code sections simultaneously — urban lot splits, ADUs, streamlined approvals, lot splits, and subdivision maps — in a single bill. AB2433 amends the Density Bonus Law to reduce the objective standards cities may apply. SB677 compresses subdivision map appeal timelines. Each bill is presented as a discrete housing production measure. Together, they constitute a structural transfer of land use authority from locally elected councils to Sacramento — bill by bill, code section by code section, eligibility deadline by eligibility deadline. The California Stewardship Fund's position is unchanged: this isn't about stopping housing. It is about who decides.

 
What to Watch
AB1710 — Permit Streamlining: Deemed Conformity
Carrillo
Mandates that cities automatically deem qualifying housing projects in conformity with local ordinances, eliminating discretionary review and the ability to enforce locally adopted development standards.
AB1751 — Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act
Quirk-Silva
Creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, removes all discretionary review, and bans cities from collecting impact fees — shifting infrastructure costs entirely to existing taxpayers.
AB2390 — Streamlined Approvals: Objective Standards
Schiavo
Amends SB 35's ministerial approval framework to further narrow the conditions cities may impose on qualifying projects, accelerating the erosion of local discretionary review authority.
AB2576 — Transit-Oriented Development
Harabedian
Amends the TOD statute to restrict the infrastructure conditions and impact fees cities may impose on transit-adjacent projects approved under mandatory ministerial review.
AB2601 — Streamlined Approval: Multiple Pathways
Lee
Expands by-right ministerial approval across five code sections at once — lot splits, ADUs, SB 35 streamlining, and subdivision maps — stripping local discretion and CEQA applicability across all of them simultaneously.
AB2074 — Regional Transit Hub Districts
Haney
Creates a state-imposed transit hub district overlay mandating ministerial approval for downtown housing projects, overriding local zoning and blocking cities from conditioning approvals on infrastructure capacity.
AB2433 — Density Bonus Law Amendment
Alvarez
Amends the Density Bonus Law to reduce the objective standards cities may apply and expand the concessions developers can demand, further restricting local discretionary review over higher-density projects.
AB748 — Preapproved Housing Plans
Harabedian
Forces cities to establish and administer preapproved housing plan programs on a state-driven timeline, compelling ministerial approval for conforming projects and stripping design review authority with no funding offset.
SB677 — Subdivision Map Act: Appeals
Wiener
Amends subdivision map appeal timelines and JPA financing procedures in ways that compress local review windows and reduce cities' discretionary leverage over subdivision approvals.
SB1117 — ADU and JADU Law
Cervantes
Amends the ADU statute to extend or strengthen state override of local zoning, likely expanding ministerial approval mandates and further reducing cities' remaining discretionary authority over accessory dwelling units.
SB722 — TOD: Excluded Parcels
Wahab
Adjusts which parcels are excluded from mandatory ministerial TOD approval — with an urgency clause that takes effect immediately, foreclosing any local planning adaptation period if it expands state preemption.
SB1014 — Preliminary Improvement Estimates
Grayson
Requires cities to deliver early-stage preliminary improvement estimates to developers, creating an unfunded administrative mandate without restricting what conditions cities may ultimately require.
 
Before You Close This

Six days. Get your planning director on record.AB1710 becomes committee-eligible on March 7. Contact your Assembly Housing and Community Development committee representatives now and ask them where they stand on a bill that makes your city's zoning ordinances advisory. This isn't about stopping housing — it's about who decides. Your planning director's documented infrastructure and service capacity concerns, submitted before the hearing is noticed, are the most credible argument Sacramento will receive.

 

Quiet weeks reveal the architecture.When no bills move and no amendments are filed, the session shows you its bones — the procedural scaffolding that makes fast action possible the moment authors are ready. Eight eligibility dates in 14 days, two blank shells that can be filled overnight, and a pattern of compounding preemptions across every housing code section in the Government Code. This is the moment to read the calendar, not wait for the gavel.
