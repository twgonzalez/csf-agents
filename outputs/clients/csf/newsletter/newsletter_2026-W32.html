WEEK OF AUGUST 3, 2026
 

Nothing moved in Sacramento this week — no votes, no amendments, no floor action. That's not a reprieve. It's the setup. Eight bills cross their committee hearing-eligibility threshold in the next 14 days, and two of them are still blank vessels that can absorb preemption language at any moment before a hearing is noticed. The calendar is the story this week, and your window to respond is closing.

Quiet week. Active clock.Eight deadlines in 14 days.the hearing calendar is the weapon.AB1710 becomes eligible for its first committee hearing in six days — March 7 — followed by AB1751 and AB1771 on March 12. Eight bills in total cross their hearing-eligibility thresholds within the next two weeks, which means the committee sequencing decisions that determine whether these bills live or die are being made right now, off the floor and out of public view. When the legislature looks quiet, it is often because the action has moved into the rooms where hearing calendars get set and spot-bill language gets drafted. This is one of those weeks.

AB1710 arrives first.Its mechanism strips conformity review.your ordinances become advisory.AB1710 is the most immediately urgent bill on the watch list, and its mechanism deserves careful attention before it lands in the Housing and Community Development Committee. The bill amends the Permit Streamlining Act to add Government Code Section 65958, which requires cities to deem qualifying housing projects automatically in conformity with applicable local ordinances and standards — effectively creating a ministerial approval pathway through a conformity fiction. That means a project that your planning staff would otherwise evaluate against your specific design standards, infrastructure capacity, and conditional use requirements gets waved through instead. The bill removes discretionary review not by abolishing it outright but by mandating a legal finding that renders it superfluous, and it imposes an unfunded compliance burden on city staff to adjudicate those deemed-conformity claims without any state offset. AB1751, which follows five days later, hits harder on infrastructure: it creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhome development on any residential parcel and prohibits cities from collecting impact fees for water, sewer, or traffic — a direct state mandate that shifts infrastructure cost-shifting entirely onto existing taxpayers.

Two bills. No language yet.Substantive preemption could drop anytime.blank shells become binding law fast.AB2127 and AB2079 are both on the eligibility horizon and both currently carry empty spot-bill summaries — meaning they exist as legislative vehicles with no substantive content yet filed. In Sacramento's procedural grammar, a spot bill is a placeholder; a gut-and-amend is the delivery mechanism. An author can drop full preemption language into either of these shells at any point before a hearing is noticed, and the public comment window that follows is often measured in days rather than weeks. This is not a theoretical risk. The same session that is advancing AB2390 — which tightens the conditions cities may apply during SB 35 ministerial review and further preempts local authority — and AB2576, which restricts what infrastructure conditions cities may impose on transit-oriented development approvals, is also carrying eight placeholder bills in active circulation. The spot-bill pipeline is how preemption language avoids early scrutiny.

The pattern is now structural.Not a bill. A strategy.local control erodes layer by layer.Taken together, the bills on this week's watch list — AB1710, AB1751, AB2390, AB2576, AB748, AB2074 — are not independent policy choices. They are a coordinated erosion strategy. AB748 forces cities to establish preapproved plan programs and removes design review for conforming projects, creating an unfunded administrative mandate with no opt-out. AB2074 creates a state-imposed regional transit hub district overlay that removes discretionary review and restricts infrastructure conditioning authority in designated areas. AB2390 narrows the objective standards cities may apply in the already-constrained SB 35 streamlined review process. Each bill removes one more lever that local governments use to ensure development is accountable to the communities that will actually absorb it. The fight is not about whether housing gets built. It is about who decides what accountability looks like — the planning commission that knows the intersection, or Sacramento.

 
What to Watch
AB1710 — Permit Streamlining Act: Housing Conformity
Carrillo
Requires cities to deem qualifying housing projects automatically in conformity with local ordinances, eliminating discretionary review and imposing unfunded compliance burdens with no local opt-out.
AB1751 — Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act
Quirk-Silva
Creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, removes all discretionary review, and prohibits cities from collecting impact fees — shifting infrastructure costs entirely to existing taxpayers.
AB748 — Preapproved Housing Plans
Harabedian
Forces cities to build and administer a state-driven preapproved plan program on Sacramento's timeline, stripping design review authority and creating an unfunded administrative mandate with no local opt-out.
AB2390 — Streamlined Approvals: Objective Standards
Schiavo
Tightens the SB 35 ministerial approval pathway by narrowing the objective standards cities may apply and limiting the conditions under which cities can push back on qualifying projects.
AB2576 — Transit-Oriented Development
Harabedian
Amends the TOD statute to further restrict the infrastructure conditions and impact fees cities may impose on qualifying transit-adjacent projects, shifting capacity costs to existing residents.
AB2074 — Regional Transit Hub Districts
Haney
Creates a state-imposed transit hub district overlay mandating ministerial approval for downtown housing developments, preempting local zoning and eliminating cities' authority to condition approvals on infrastructure upgrades.
AB2433 — Density Bonus Law Amendment
Alvarez
Expands Government Code Section 65915 to further restrict local discretion over density bonus projects, broadening the waivers and concessions cities must grant beyond locally adopted standards.
SB677 — Subdivision Map Act Appeals
Wiener
Amends subdivision map appeal procedures to compress timelines and restrict grounds for local denial, reducing cities' discretionary review authority over subdivision approvals.
