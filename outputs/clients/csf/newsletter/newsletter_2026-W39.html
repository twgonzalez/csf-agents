Spot bills are Sacramento's loaded gun. Here's how the mechanism works.                                                                                          
Local Control Intelligence
WEEK OF SEPTEMBER 21, 2026
 

No votes this week. No amendments. The Capitol looked quiet — and that's exactly when you need to be paying attention. Eight bills cross their committee hearing-eligibility threshold in the next 14 days, and two of them are still blank placeholders that can absorb sweeping preemption language before anyone notices. Quiet weeks aren't rest periods. They're loading time.

The calendar, not the vote.Eight deadlines in fourteen days.the window closes without you.Nothing moved through a committee this week. No author filed amendments. By every surface measure, the Capitol went quiet — but the clock didn't stop. AB1710, which directly preempts local authority by freezing the zoning standards cities can apply mid-review, becomes eligible for its first committee hearing in six days, on March 7. AB1751, which eliminates all discretionary review and strips cities of impact fee authority for townhome development statewide, follows five days later on March 12. Eight bills in total cross their hearing-eligibility threshold within the next 14 days. The quiet week is the window before the window closes.

Blank text, real danger.Spot bills don't advertise themselves.preemption language drops without warning.Two bills with upcoming eligibility dates — AB2127 on accessory dwelling units and AB2079 on adaptive reuse — currently carry empty spot-bill summaries: boilerplate text, no substantive content, no declared mechanism. That is not an accident and it is not an oversight. It is how Sacramento inserts sweeping local control preemption into the legislative record after the public has stopped watching. An author can gut a spot bill and insert new language at any moment before a hearing is noticed, and neither the public nor the affected city has a formal right to advance notice. The same pattern appears in SB1090, titled 'Housing: disaster impacted areas,' which sits at the printer stage with nothing more than 'an act relating to housing' in its summary — disaster recovery framing is a historically reliable vehicle for zoning preemption that would otherwise face opposition.

The fee ban is the point.Infrastructure doesn't build itself.existing taxpayers absorb the entire bill.AB1751 is the sharpest example of how infrastructure cost-shifting works in practice, and it deserves a full accounting before its March 12 eligibility date arrives. The bill creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhome development on any residential parcel, removes discretionary review entirely, and — critically — bars cities from charging water, sewer, traffic, or school impact fees for qualifying projects. That last clause is the fiscal gut punch: every new townhome resident connected to a city's water system, every child enrolled in a school district, every car added to a local street gets there without the developer paying a proportionate share of the infrastructure cost. SB1014, which requires cities to issue preliminary improvement estimates on demand during pre-application review, compounds the problem by anchoring developer expectations early and giving applicants a paper trail to challenge conditions imposed later — before the full scope of project impacts is even known. The cost doesn't disappear. It shifts to the people already living there.

One session. One direction.The architecture is already built.who decides is the only question left.AB1710 locks in zoning standards at application date, preventing cities from updating objective standards mid-review. AB2601 and SB1116 expand the by-right ministerial approval pathways that already strip discretionary review from housing decisions. SB677 compresses subdivision map appeal windows, narrowing the leverage cities have to negotiate developer-funded infrastructure as a condition of map approval. AB2074 designates regional transit hub districts where local height limits, density standards, and general plan designations are effectively suspended by state mandate. These bills don't share an author. They don't share a committee. But they share a mechanism: each one removes a specific tool that local governments use to ensure development fits the community that will absorb it. California Stewardship Fund's work this session is to name that pattern clearly, document it in the legislative record, and make sure every planning director, council member, and engaged resident understands what is being taken — and from whom.

 
What to Watch
AB1710 — Permit Streamlining Act: housing development projects
Carrillo
Preempts local authority by barring cities from applying updated zoning standards to any housing application deemed complete — freezing the regulatory clock mid-review and stripping cities of the discretion to respond to changed conditions.
AB1751 — Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act
Quirk-Silva
Creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, removes all discretionary review, and bans cities from charging impact fees for water, sewer, traffic, or schools — shifting infrastructure costs entirely to existing taxpayers.
AB2601 — Planning and zoning: streamlined approval and subdivisions
Lee
Expands state-mandated ministerial approval pathways under core streamlining and subdivision statutes, further restricting the conditions cities may apply and the standards they may enforce on qualifying housing projects.
SB1116 — Planning and zoning: housing development projects: subdivisions
Caballero
Expands by-right housing approval pathways, restricts HOA and local conditioning authority, and limits cities' ability to require infrastructure improvements proportionate to new subdivision development.
SB1117 — Accessory dwelling units and junior accessory dwelling units
Cervantes
Expands the scope of ADUs and JADUs that must receive ministerial approval, preempting local zoning and eliminating discretionary review while adding infrastructure pressure cities cannot recover through impact fees or conditions.
AB2074 — Regional transit hub districts: downtown housing developments
Haney
Creates a state-mandated by-right approval pathway for downtown development in designated transit hub districts, suspending local height limits, density standards, and CEQA review while limiting cities' authority to require infrastructure contributions.
SB677 — Housing financing: joint powers agreements: subdivisions: maps: appeals
Wiener
Compresses subdivision map appeal timelines and expands JPA housing bond authority in ways that erode local discretionary review and reduce cities' leverage to condition map approvals on developer-funded infrastructure.
SB1014 — Development projects: preliminary estimate of required improvements
Grayson
Requires cities to issue formal preliminary improvement estimates on demand during pre-application review, creating compliance burdens and giving applicants a paper trail to challenge infrastructure conditions imposed later in the entitlement process.
AB2433 — Housing development: density bonus
Alvarez
Amends state Density Bonus Law to expand the density increases and concessions cities must grant by right, further preempting local zoning and increasing unit counts without restoring cities' authority to require proportional infrastructure contributions.
AB748 — Single-family and multifamily housing units: preapproved plans
Harabedian
Forces cities to establish and administer state-mandated preapproved housing plan programs that compress local design review and impose unfunded administrative obligations with no opt-out mechanism.
 
Before You Close This

Six days. Contact your Assembly member today.AB1710 is eligible for its first committee hearing on March 7. Call your Assembly member's district office and ask them directly: do you support letting cities apply updated zoning standards during active project review, or does Sacramento decide that too? This isn't about stopping housing — it's about who decides. That question deserves an answer before the hearing is noticed.

 

The pattern is legible.Quiet legislative weeks are not pauses — they are the interval between the loading and the firing. Eight bills reach committee eligibility in the next 14 days, spot bills can absorb new language at any moment, and the infrastructure cost-shifting mechanisms are already written into enrolled law. The work now is documentation, coordination, and pressure — before the hearings are noticed and the window closes.
