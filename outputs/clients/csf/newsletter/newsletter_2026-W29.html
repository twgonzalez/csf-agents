WEEK OF JULY 13, 2026
 

No votes were cast in Sacramento this week. No amendments were filed. The calendar was quiet — and that's exactly when you need to be paying attention. Eight bills cross their committee hearing-eligibility threshold in the next 14 days, two of them carrying empty placeholder summaries that could be loaded with preemption language before anyone outside the Capitol knows what's coming. The silence isn't a pause. It's the intake of breath before the next round.

The calendar looks quiet.The deadlines are not.eight bills, fourteen days.Nothing moved a legislative stage this week. No amendments were filed. By the standard metrics, this was a slow week in Sacramento — and that framing is precisely what makes the next 14 days dangerous. AB1710, which forces cities to deem qualifying housing projects automatically in conformity with local ordinances regardless of actual local standards, becomes eligible for its first committee hearing in six days, on March 7. AB1751, the Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act, follows five days later. Eight bills in total cross their hearing-eligibility threshold within this window, and once a bill is noticed for hearing, the amendment window that could have shaped it has largely closed.

Two bills. No text.Hearing eligibility approaching.preemption drafted behind closed doors.The most underreported risk on this week's calendar isn't AB1710 or AB1751 — it's AB2127 and AB2079, both carrying empty spot-bill summaries as their hearing-eligibility dates close in. A spot bill with a housing title and a one-line 'act relating to housing' summary is a legislative container. Substantive preemption language — the kind that removes discretionary review, eliminates impact fee authority, or mandates ministerial approval statewide — can be inserted into either bill at any moment before the hearing is noticed. The public, the planning commission, and the city attorney's office will have days, not weeks, to respond. AB2390, which tightens the conditions under which cities may push back on SB 35-qualifying projects, and SB722, which narrows the parcel exclusions cities may invoke under the Transit-Oriented Communities framework, illustrate exactly what that language looks like once it arrives.

Cities approve the project.Cities absorb the cost.existing taxpayers fill the gap.The fiscal mechanism running through this session's watch list is not incidental — it is the design. AB1751 bars cities from charging water, sewer, traffic, or school impact fees for new townhome residents, shifting infrastructure costs entirely onto existing ratepayers and taxpayers with no state offset. AB2601 compounds the problem by extending ministerial approval tracks across urban lot splits, ADUs, and subdivision maps simultaneously, preventing cities from conditioning any of those approvals on the infrastructure improvements the projects will require. AB2074's regional transit hub district overlay restricts cities from requiring developers to fund utility upgrades or traffic capacity improvements as a condition of approval. SB1014 requires cities to produce preliminary infrastructure cost estimates for developers early in the entitlement process — a transparency measure that sounds reasonable until you recognize it creates staffing and systems obligations with no identified funding to meet them. The pattern is consistent: state mandate, local bill, existing residents pay.

This is not a coincidence.It is a legislative strategy.who decides how California grows.Taken bill by bill, this session looks like a series of incremental technical adjustments to the Government Code. Taken together, it is a systematic transfer of land use authority from elected local bodies to Sacramento. AB1710 preempts local authority over development conformity determinations. AB1751 removes discretionary review from residential parcels statewide. AB2576 and SB722 expand the ministerial footprint near transit. AB2433 broadens the density bonus framework that already limits what conditions cities may impose. AB748 mandates that cities establish and administer preapproved plan programs on a state-driven timeline with no opt-out and no funding. The California Stewardship Fund's position is not that these projects shouldn't be built. It is that the communities where they are built — and the taxpayers who will fund the infrastructure to serve them — have a right to be part of the decision. That right is what this session is systematically removing.

 
What to Watch
AB1710 — Permit Streamlining: Deemed Conformity
Carrillo
Forces cities to treat qualifying housing projects as automatically conforming with local ordinances, eliminating discretionary review and creating an unfunded ministerial approval mandate with no local opt-out.
AB1751 — Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act
Quirk-Silva
Creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, preempts local authority over zoning and design review, and bars cities from charging impact fees — shifting all infrastructure costs to existing taxpayers.
AB2390 — Streamlined Approvals: Objective Standards
Schiavo
Narrows the objective standards cities may apply during SB 35 ministerial review, further removing the conditions and modifications cities use to make state-mandated projects fit local infrastructure and neighborhood context.
AB2576 — Transit-Oriented Development
Harabedian
Expands the ministerial, by-right TOD approval framework and likely restricts the impact fees and infrastructure conditions cities may impose on qualifying transit-adjacent projects.
AB2601 — Streamlined Approval: Multiple Pathways
Lee
Simultaneously expands ministerial approval tracks across urban lot splits, ADUs, SB 35 streamlining, and subdivision maps, removing local discretion and infrastructure conditioning authority across five code sections at once.
SB722 — Transit-Oriented Housing: Excluded Parcels
Wahab
Narrows the parcel exclusions cities may use to opt sites out of the Transit-Oriented Communities ministerial approval framework, expanding state preemption of local zoning without community input.
AB2074 — Regional Transit Hub Districts
Haney
Creates a new state-imposed transit hub district overlay that mandates ministerial housing approval in downtown areas, preempts local zoning, eliminates discretionary review, and bars cities from requiring infrastructure improvements as a condition of approval.
AB2433 — Density Bonus Law Expansion
Alvarez
Expands the Density Bonus Law framework that already strips local zoning authority, likely broadening eligible projects, increasing required density multipliers, and reducing the conditions cities may attach to approvals.
AB748 — Preapproved Housing Plans Mandate
Harabedian
Compels cities to establish and maintain preapproved plan programs on a state-driven timeline, reduces design review authority for conforming projects, and imposes unfunded administrative costs with no opt-out mechanism.
SB1014 — Preliminary Infrastructure Estimate Requirement
Grayson
Requires cities to provide developers with early preliminary estimates of required infrastructure improvements, creating unfunded staffing and administrative obligations without restricting what cities may ultimately require.
SB677 — Housing Finance: JPA Bonds and Map Appeals
Wiener
Amends subdivision map appeal timelines and JPA financing authority in ways that compress local review windows and reduce city leverage over project conditions during subdivision approvals.
 
Before You Close This

Six days. Call before the hearing is set.AB1710 becomes eligible for committee hearing on March 7. Contact your Assembly member and the members of the Housing and Community Development Committee now — before the hearing is noticed and the amendment window closes. Ask them directly where they stand on deemed-conformity mandates that strip cities of the ability to apply their own ordinances. This isn't about stopping housing — it's about who decides. A 90-second call this week is worth more than a formal comment letter filed after the hearing date is set.

 

Quiet weeks are working weeks.The bills that remake California's planning landscape rarely announce themselves with drama. They move through eligibility windows, get noticed for committee, and arrive at a vote before most city halls have finished drafting their comment letters. Your advantage is knowing the calendar before Sacramento counts on you not to. Use it.
