WEEK OF AUGUST 31, 2026
 

The quietest weeks in Sacramento are often the most instructive. No votes, no amendments — but eight bills are about to become hearable, and two of them are still blank. That's not a coincidence. That's a tactic. Here's what your staff needs to understand before March 7.

Nothing moved this week.Eight deadlines are coming.the blank bills are the story.The Capitol went quiet this week — no floor votes, no author amendments, no stage changes. But quiet weeks in Sacramento have a shape, and this one has a specific geometry: eight bills cross their committee hearing eligibility thresholds in the next 14 days, with AB1710 becoming eligible March 7 and AB1751 and AB1771 following five days later. That's the foreground. The background is more interesting. AB2127 and AB2079 are both carrying empty spot-bill summaries right now — boilerplate language, no operative text — and both will be eligible for hearing before the month is out. A gut-and-amend can drop substantive preemption language into either shell with no advance notice to your planning department or your city attorney.

Spot bills aren't placeholders.They're loaded chambers.pointed at discretionary review.The spot-bill tactic is not procedural housekeeping — it is a deliberate sequencing strategy. SB1090, titled 'Housing: disaster impacted areas,' carries only the phrase 'An act relating to housing.' AB1899, titled 'Office of Youth Homelessness Prevention,' is equally bare. These titles are sympathetic by design; the operative text that follows will not be. Once substantive language drops into any of these shells, the clock to a hearing can compress to days. The bills most immediately before a committee — AB1710, which adds Section 65958 to the Government Code to lock in zoning standards as of application completeness and directly preempts local authority to apply updated objective standards mid-review, and AB1751, which creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel and removes discretionary review entirely — both represent the mature version of what the spot bills are gestating. Your city's planning director needs to be watching both the calendar and the shell.

Design review gets the headline.Infrastructure costs absorb the blow.your public works budget, not Sacramento's.The fiscal mechanism buried inside these bills rarely makes the press release. AB1751 is the clearest current example: it creates a ministerial approval pathway for townhome development on any residential parcel across the state, but the provision that will land hardest on your finance director is the impact fee prohibition. Cities cannot charge water, sewer, traffic, or school impact fees for new townhome residents under the bill's framework — infrastructure cost-shifting from developers to existing taxpayers, written directly into state law. AB2601 and SB1116, both enrolled, work the same vein: subdivision streamlining under Government Code Sections 66411.7 and 66499.41 limits cities' ability to condition approvals on infrastructure improvements, and the addition of Civil Code Section 714.9 in SB1116 extends preemption into HOA and deed restriction territory. SB1014 adds an ironic footnote — it requires cities to produce preliminary estimates of required improvements early in the review process, an unfunded administrative obligation that now sits alongside a growing body of law stripping cities of the authority to actually require those improvements.

This session has a strategy.Spot bills fill the gaps.local control erodes bill by bill.Step back from any single bill and the session pattern becomes legible. The enrolled bills — AB2074 forcing ministerial approval at regional transit hubs, SB1361 expanding which transit agencies trigger that same by-right pathway, AB2601 and SB1116 streamlining subdivisions — represent one layer of preemption already locked in. The bills approaching eligibility — AB1710, AB1751 — represent the next layer in active motion. The spot bills represent the layer that hasn't been written yet but will be, on a timeline designed to minimize the window for organized local response. California Stewardship Fund's formal comment letters on AB1710 and AB1751 are in preparation; planning directors and city attorneys who want to coordinate on documented infrastructure and service capacity concerns should be in contact with our staff before March 7. This isn't about stopping housing. It is about whether the people closest to these decisions get to make them.

 
What to Watch
AB1710 — Permit Streamlining Act: Housing Conformity
Carrillo
Preempts local authority to apply updated zoning or objective standards to housing applications once deemed complete, freezing cities' regulatory discretion mid-review and creating immediate compliance exposure for planning departments.
AB1751 — Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act
Quirk-Silva
Creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, removes discretionary review entirely, and bars cities from collecting impact fees for water, sewer, traffic, or schools — shifting all infrastructure costs to existing taxpayers.
AB2601 — Streamlined Approval and Subdivisions
Lee
Expands by-right and ministerial approval pathways under SB 9 and SB 35 frameworks, overriding local zoning and eliminating discretionary review while limiting cities' authority to condition subdivision approvals on infrastructure improvements.
SB1116 — Housing Development Projects: Subdivisions
Caballero
Imposes ministerial approval mandates on housing and subdivision projects, preempts HOA and deed restrictions via Civil Code Section 714.9, and limits cities' ability to condition approvals on infrastructure capacity upgrades.
AB2074 — Regional Transit Hub Districts: Downtown Housing
Haney
Forces ministerial approval of 'downtown housing developments' in state-defined transit hub districts, overriding general plans, specific plans, and local zoning with no infrastructure conditioning authority for cities.
SB1361 — Transit-Oriented Housing: Local Governments
Durazo
Expands the pool of transit agencies and projects triggering mandatory ministerial housing approval under Government Code Section 65912.157, stripping more cities of zoning and discretionary review authority without restoring infrastructure mitigation tools.
AB956 — ADUs and JADUs
Quirk-Silva
Further narrows local authority to condition ADU and JADU approvals on design, fees, or infrastructure requirements, and nullifies HOA deed restrictions communities rely on to manage density impacts.
AB748 — Single-Family and Multifamily Preapproved Plans
Harabedian
Mandates that cities establish and administer preapproved housing plan programs, creating a state-directed ministerial approval track that compresses discretionary design review and imposes unfunded compliance obligations on local planning departments.
AB2433 — Density Bonus Law Expansion
Alvarez
Amends the Density Bonus Law to further deepen state preemption of local zoning, compel approvals at densities beyond community plans, and limit cities' ability to condition projects on infrastructure improvements.
SB677 — Subdivision Map Act: Appeals and Bond Approvals
Wiener
Tightens subdivision map appeal timelines and expands state-level housing finance authority, narrowing the procedural window cities have to impose conditions or deny subdivisions and reducing local leverage over project approvals.
