Spot bills are Sacramento's oldest trick. Here's what's hiding inside them.                                                                                          
Local Control Intelligence
WEEK OF SEPTEMBER 28, 2026
 

The legislature went quiet this week — no votes, no amendments, no floor action. Don't mistake stillness for safety. Eight bills cross their first committee hearing threshold within 14 days, and two of them are still empty shells that can be loaded with preemption language at any moment. This is the week to understand how the machinery works before it starts moving.

Nothing moved. Watch anyway.The deadlines are accelerating.empty bills fill fast.A quiet week in Sacramento is not a safe week. No bills advanced a legislative stage and no amendments were filed — but eight bills cross their committee hearing eligibility thresholds in the next 14 days, with AB1710 becoming eligible in six days on March 7 and AB1751 following five days later. More urgent still: AB2127 and AB2079 are currently carrying empty spot-bill summaries with no substantive language, meaning preemption provisions can be inserted into either bill at any moment before their hearings are noticed. This is how Sacramento moves fastest — not through floor drama, but through procedural windows your staff isn't watching.

Blank titles. Real consequences.Spot bills aren't placeholders.they're loaded at the last moment.The spot bill is Sacramento's most underappreciated tool. SB1016, titled 'Transitional housing bond,' currently reads only 'An act relating to housing' — parked in the Rules Committee holding pattern that signals it hasn't been written yet. SB1090, AB1899, and AB1924 follow the same pattern: real bill numbers, real hearing eligibility dates, and no substantive content. That will change. When language drops into a spot bill, the public notice window is short, the committee calendar is already set, and local agencies rarely have time to mobilize testimony. AB1710 — the Permit Streamlining Act amendment that preempts local authority to apply updated zoning standards mid-review — shows what these vehicles become: a direct prohibition on your planning department doing its job during live applications.

Estimates. Estoppel. Infrastructure.SB1014 reframes who controls conditions.your leverage disappears early.While the headline bills attract attention, SB1014 (Grayson) is doing quieter damage to the fiscal authority cities depend on. The bill adds Government Code Section 65941.3 to require local agencies to produce a preliminary estimate of required on-site and off-site improvements early in the development review process — before full project impacts are known. That estimate, once delivered, becomes the baseline applicants use to challenge any infrastructure condition imposed later in entitlement. There is no state funding to offset the administrative cost of generating these estimates, and no statutory language clarifying they are non-binding. Paired with AB1751's outright prohibition on impact fees for townhome development — a state mandate that removes discretionary review and shifts infrastructure costs entirely onto existing taxpayers — SB1014 completes a two-part squeeze: eliminate the fees cities can charge, then anchor the improvements they can require to a preliminary number issued before the full scope of the project is known.

Pattern, not coincidence.Session logic is cumulative.local control erodes bill by bill.AB2601, SB1116, SB1117, and AB2074 are all enrolled — they passed. Each one removed discretionary review, created ministerial approval pathways, or restricted the infrastructure conditions cities can impose as a price of that approval. The bills moving toward eligibility now — AB1751, AB1710, and whatever lands inside the open spot bills — are not a new wave. They are the continuation of a session strategy that advances local control preemption across every housing type simultaneously: townhomes, ADUs, transit-adjacent apartments, downtown developments, and subdivisions. CSF's mission is to hold the line on the principle that the people closest to a decision should make it. That principle is not at risk in one bill. It is being dismantled one eligibility window at a time.

 
What to Watch
AB1710 — Permit Streamlining Act: Housing Ordinances
Carrillo
Prohibits cities from applying updated zoning standards to housing applications already deemed complete, freezing local authority mid-review and removing discretionary leverage over project conditions.
AB1751 — Missing Middle Townhome Ownership Act
Quirk-Silva
Creates a statewide ministerial pathway for townhomes on any residential parcel, eliminates all discretionary review, and bans cities from charging impact fees — shifting infrastructure costs entirely to existing taxpayers.
SB1014 — Development Projects: Preliminary Improvement Estimates
Grayson
Requires cities to issue early improvement estimates that applicants can use to challenge infrastructure conditions imposed later, indirectly capping cities' cost-recovery authority before full project impacts are known.
AB2601 — Streamlined Approval and Subdivisions
Lee
Expands by-right ministerial approvals across core streamlining and subdivision statutes, further restricting the conditions cities may apply and limiting infrastructure requirements on qualifying projects.
SB1116 — Housing Development Projects: Subdivisions
Caballero
Expands ministerial approval pathways for housing and subdivisions, strips HOA and local conditioning authority, and limits cities' ability to tie approvals to infrastructure capacity.
SB1117 — ADUs and Junior ADUs
Cervantes
Expands the categories of ADUs and JADUs that must receive by-right approval, preempts local zoning and design standards, and increases unit approvals without restoring cities' impact fee authority.
AB2074 — Regional Transit Hub Districts: Downtown Housing
Haney
Creates a state-mandated ministerial approval pathway for downtown housing in transit hub districts, overriding local height limits, density standards, and general plans while limiting infrastructure conditioning authority.
SB677 — Subdivision Map Act: Appeals and JPA Bond Authority
Wiener
Compresses local appeal windows for subdivision maps and expands joint powers housing finance authority in ways that may bypass local voter approval, reducing cities' leverage over infrastructure conditions.
AB2433 — Housing Development: Density Bonus
Alvarez
Amends the State Density Bonus Law to expand mandatory density increases and concession rights cities must grant, overriding local zoning and adding capacity pressure without restoring infrastructure fee authority.
AB748 — Single-Family and Multifamily: Preapproved Plans
Harabedian
Compels cities to establish and administer preapproved housing plan programs that create ministerial-leaning approval tracks, curtailing design review and imposing unfunded administrative obligations with no local opt-out.
